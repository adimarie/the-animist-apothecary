Overview

The book in one sentence

The Living Elements is a 12-chapter practical guide that teaches the elements (fire, water, earth, air, and the fifth and beyond) as living beings, not as symbols, and walks the reader through a year of ceremonial practice that builds genuine relationship with each.

The book in one paragraph

Most Western readers have been taught to think of fire, water, earth, and air as either chemistry or as symbolic correspondences in a magical system. The Living Elements makes a different claim. The elements are beings: ensouled, named, in possession of intelligence and agency, and capable of relationship with a human who approaches with the right protocols. The book teaches those protocols. Drawing on California and Mexican animist traditions, on the author's own ceremonial practice, and on the broader genealogy of animist thought from David Abram and Stephen Harrod Buhner to Robin Wall Kimmerer, The Living Elements offers a chapter-per-element deep practice. By the end of the book, the reader has built a home altar, has made a first offering at each element, has tended a relationship with a plant ally chosen for each element, and has performed at least one solo seasonal ceremony.

What makes this book different

Four distinctions separate this book from existing work in the genre.

First, the elements are treated as beings, not symbols. Most existing books in modern witchcraft, ceremonial magic, and even much of the animist literature treat the elements as categories of correspondence (fire = passion, water = emotion, earth = grounding, air = thought). This book refuses that frame. The elements have names. They have temperaments. They prefer certain offerings and not others. They communicate. The book teaches the reader to listen.

Second, plants are integrated as ceremonial allies throughout. Each chapter introduces a plant ally local to California, the Southwest, and Mexico, with botanical identification, ceremonial use, and sourcing ethics. Readers who finish the book have met thirteen plant allies, not as remedies but as bridges between the human and elemental worlds.

Third, the book takes an animist position on Western practice that is principled, not nostalgic. The author is a non-Indigenous practitioner who has trained in Mexican curandera and California native traditions. The book is honest about lineage, attribution, and the difference between transmission and appropriation. It is not a "shamanism for white people" book. It is a book about how Western-rooted practitioners can build their own animist practice with integrity.

Fourth, the structure is a year of practice, not a survey of ideas. Each chapter ends with daily, weekly, and lunation-cycle practices. The book is meant to be read with a candle lit, water near, soil on the desk. It assumes the reader will follow the practices. By chapter twelve, the reader has built a working ceremonial life.

Why this book now

Three forces have prepared the audience. The animist worldview has moved from academic philosophy (David Abram's The Spell of the Sensuous, 1996) into mass-market memoir (Robin Wall Kimmerer's Braiding Sweetgrass, 2013, three-million-plus copies sold) and now into popular ceremonial practice (Sophie Strand's The Flowering Wand, 2022, Inner Traditions, top-50 esoteric category for 18 months). The reader who finished Kimmerer and Strand and Foor's Ancestral Medicine is asking: how do I actually do this? The genre has been long on worldview and short on practice. The Living Elements answers the practice question directly.

The climate and ecological emergency has also accelerated the search. Readers who used to find spiritual practice in yoga or in mindfulness are increasingly looking for traditions that put them in relationship with the more-than-human world. The animist frame is the natural home for that search.

And the post-pandemic ceremonial revival is now five years deep, with online cohort schools (Temple of Witchcraft, Ancestral Medicine, Modern Witch University, Irish Pagan School) demonstrating an audience that is willing to pay for sustained practice instruction. The Living Elements is the trade book that this audience reads before it enrolls in a school.

Comparable titles

1. Daniel Foor, Ancestral Medicine (North Atlantic Books, 2017)

Sales: 50,000+ copies, eight years in print. Foor's book launched a practitioner movement and seeded the certification-school model that Ancestral Medicine (the organization) now runs.

Similar: Same animist frame, same practitioner-track structure, same author-school model behind the book.
Different: The elements, not ancestors. Foor's book is the entry text for ancestral healing. The Living Elements is the entry text for elemental relationship. They are complementary, not competing.

2. Stephen Harrod Buhner, Plant Intelligence and the Imaginal Realm (Bear & Company / Inner Traditions, 2014)

Sales: 30,000+ copies. Buhner is the foundational thinker on non-human intelligence in the contemporary plant-medicine lane.

Similar: The claim that non-human entities possess intelligence and can be known directly.
Different: Practice over philosophy. Buhner's book is 600 pages of argument. The Living Elements is 250 to 300 pages of practice. The two books make the same claim; one persuades, the other instructs.

3. Sophie Strand, The Flowering Wand (Inner Traditions, 2022)

Sales: 60,000+ copies estimated. Strand is the contemporary voice of mythopoetic animism with the largest crossover audience in the lane.

Similar: Rooted in mythopoetic and ecological framing, written for the reader who is already past beginner.
Different: Explicitly practical. Strand offers vision; The Living Elements offers ceremony.

4. Robin Wall Kimmerer, Braiding Sweetgrass (Milkweed Editions, 2013)

Sales: 3,000,000+ copies cumulative. The single most influential animist book of the 21st century.

Similar: Takes the animist worldview seriously and translates it into practical terms.
Different: Written from inside Western ceremonial practice. Kimmerer is Potawatomi and writes from Indigenous lineage; The Living Elements is written by a non-Indigenous practitioner and is honest about that location. The book directly addresses the question "what does it mean to practice animism if I am not Indigenous?"

5. Mat Auryn, Psychic Witch (Llewellyn, 2020)

Sales: 250,000+ copies, the bestselling new witchcraft title of the 2020s.

Similar: Practical-practice book by a working practitioner, structured for daily use.
Different: Animist and elemental in focus, plant-integrated throughout, deeper at the practitioner end.

Chapter outline

Twelve chapters, each opening with a brief narrative passage and closing with a structured practice section.

Part One: The frame (chapters 1 to 3)

Chapter 1: The Elements Are Beings, Not Symbols

The opening claim of the book. Why most Western readers have been taught wrong, what changed when the author met fire for the first time, and what the reader is being asked to consider for the next twelve chapters. Practice: a four-day candle vigil. Sample chapter included below.

Chapter 2: How We Forgot

A brief history of Western disenchantment. The early modern shift from animate to mechanical cosmology. The cost of that shift. Why the elements stopped being beings in Western thought, and what kinds of practice survived in folk traditions, in the Hermetic ceremonial lineage, and in colonized animist cultures. Practice: a written history of the reader's own family relationship with fire and water across generations.

Chapter 3: Approach: Protocol, Offering, Listening

How an animist actually approaches a non-human being. The three discipline foundations: protocol (the rules of approach), offering (what we give), and listening (the receptive skill, harder than it sounds). Practice: a structured listening exercise at a body of water.

Part Two: The four elements (chapters 4 to 7)

Chapter 4: Fire — The First Conversation

Fire as the most accessible element for a Western practitioner. The history of fire-keeping across animist traditions (Hestia, Brigid, Mexican curandera fire ceremonies). What fire prefers as an offering. How fire communicates. Plant ally: yerba santa. Practice: a 28-day morning and evening candle vigil with daily journaling. Sample chapter included below.

Chapter 5: Water — The Listening Element

Water as the element that teaches receptivity. Sources of water-relationship across traditions (Mami Wata, the Celtic goddesses of wells, the Mexican water curandera tradition). Why most practitioners approach water with the wrong question. What water asks of us in the age of drought. Plant ally: rose. Practice: weekly visit to a body of moving water.

Chapter 6: Earth — The Patient Teacher

Earth as the element that teaches reciprocity and time. The mistake of "grounding" as Western shorthand for any earth contact. Earth-listening, earth-offering, the practice of bare-skin contact with soil. The relationship between earth practice and ancestor practice. Plant ally: mugwort (root). Practice: a sustained relationship with one specific tree across the chapter.

Chapter 7: Air — Voice and Breath

Air as the element of communication, transmission, and breath. Air-listening (the most under-developed elemental skill in modern practice). The traditions of voice as offering. Air-ethics in an age of polluted skies. Plant ally: yerba buena. Practice: daily breath ceremony at dawn.

Part Three: Beyond the four, toward a life (chapters 8 to 12)

Chapter 8: The Fifth and Beyond — Spirit, Star, Dark

The element that holds the other four (named variously: spirit, ether, akasha, the void). The elements beyond four in specific traditions. How a Western practitioner can engage these without claiming what is not theirs. Plant ally: tobacco. Practice: a night ceremony.

Chapter 9: Reciprocity — What the Elements Ask of Us

The ethical chapter. What we owe in return for relationship. The danger of the "transactional" frame and what genuine reciprocity looks like instead. Specific contemporary obligations: water protection, land stewardship, fire respect, air clarity. Practice: a written reciprocity inventory.

Chapter 10: The Home Altar

The practical chapter on physical infrastructure. How to build and maintain an elemental altar at home. Materials, placement, cleaning, what does and does not belong. The relationship between altar and offering. Practice: build your altar in the week of reading this chapter.

Chapter 11: Designing a Ceremony

The anatomy of effective ritual: opening, work, closing, integration. Why most rituals fail. The difference between ceremony for one, for a small group, and for a public. Plant ally: cacao (devotional). Practice: design and perform your own seasonal ceremony aligned with the next solstice or equinox.

Chapter 12: A Life with the Elements

Closing chapter. What it looks like to live with elemental relationship at the center of an ordinary modern life. How the practice changes over years. The invitation to continue beyond the book. Practice: a yearlong practice audit and a written letter to your future self.

Sample Chapter One

The Elements Are Beings, Not Symbols

The first time fire spoke to me, I did not know that it was speaking. I had lit a candle on the morning of the spring equinox in my grandmother's kitchen in Boyle Heights, and I had said the words I had been taught to say. I knew that the words mattered. I did not know that the candle did.

I sat with the candle for forty minutes, and then I blew it out, and then I went on with my day. Three days later I went back to the candle to relight it, and I noticed that the wick had moved. The wax had melted unevenly on one side. The candle was leaning. It had been straight when I lit it. It had been straight when I blew it out. Now it was leaning toward the east, toward the window, where I had spent the forty minutes sitting.

I told my grandmother. She did not look up from the bowl she was cleaning. Mija, she said, ya sabe que estás aquí. It knows you are here.

I was nineteen. It took me almost a decade to understand what she had said.

What this book asks of you

You are about to spend a year with the four elements. By the time you finish this book, if you do the practices, you will know fire the way you know an old friend whose moods you can read across a room. You will know water the way you know the voice of someone you love on the phone, even when you cannot hear the words. You will know earth the way you know the weight of your own body when you wake up. You will know air the way you know the difference between a kind silence and a hostile one in a room of people.

Before that can happen, I have to ask you something hard. I have to ask you to consider that the elements are not symbols.

For most of the readers of this book, this will be the strangest sentence so far. Most of us in the modern West have been taught that fire stands for passion, that water stands for emotion, that earth stands for grounding, and that air stands for thought. We have been taught this in our witchcraft books and in our astrology classes and in our magical-correspondence tables. We have been taught that the elements are categories. We have been taught that they are useful labels for psychological or symbolic content.

I am asking you to set that down.

I am not asking you to forget it. The correspondence tradition is genuine, and it is useful, and you can return to it. I am asking you to set it down for the next year so that you can meet the elements as they actually are. Which is to say: as beings. As ensouled, named, intelligent, willing, and present beings, with whom a human can enter relationship, and from whom a human can learn, and to whom a human owes something in return.

This claim will sound, to some of you, religious. It is. The animist tradition is a religious tradition. It is older than any of the religions that came after it. It survived in the corners of European folk practice and in unbroken form across most of the rest of the world, and it is the tradition that I write from. If you are reading this book, some part of you already suspects what I am about to spend twelve chapters arguing. That is why you bought the book.

What it cost me to learn this

I want to be honest about something at the start. I did not come to this practice cleanly. I did not have an unbroken lineage handed to me. My grandmother knew what she knew, and she did not teach me directly. She did not believe in teaching directly. She believed in showing up to feed me and to tell me a story when I needed one, and the practices were inside the stories, but only if I went back to them years later and found them.

So I came to the elements through a long crooked path. I learned candle work from a Mexican curandera in East LA when I was twenty-three, and I learned water ceremony from a Yaqui-trained practitioner I will not name, and I learned the four-direction altar from a Lakota relative of a friend who taught me very carefully and very slowly because she was teaching me at the edge of what could be taught to a non-Indigenous person. I learned the rest from my own practice, from showing up to the candle every morning and to the river every Sunday, and from the elements themselves, who taught me when I was patient enough to listen.

Some of what I will write in this book is mine. Some of it is borrowed, and I will name where I borrowed it. Some of it is in the air now, in the way that practices move from teacher to teacher and from culture to culture, and I will try to be honest about that too.

The elements do not belong to any tradition. The elements belong to themselves. But the ceremonies belong to the people who keep them, and I will hold that distinction throughout the book.

What "the elements are beings" actually means

When I say the elements are beings, I mean four things specifically.

First, they are named. Fire has names in every animist tradition that has met it. The name matters because the name is the beginning of the address. You do not call a stranger "human." You call them by their name, or you ask. In the early chapters of this book I will not give you names for the elements, because the names that work for me may not be the names that work for you. By the end of the book, you will have your own.

Second, they have temperament. Fire is not a category. Fire has moods. There is a fire that is patient and there is a fire that is impatient. There is a fire that is teaching and there is a fire that is angry. Most modern practitioners do not develop the perception to tell these apart, and so they treat all fires the same, and so they get the same generic response back, which is no response. Temperament is the second thing to learn, after presence.

Third, they communicate. Communication is not always linguistic. The candle on my grandmother's kitchen table leaned to the east because I had been sitting to its east for forty minutes. The next time I sat with that candle, I sat to the west. The candle leaned to the west. Communication is what I am describing. You can call it physics if you want. The physics is also true. The physics does not exhaust the relationship.

Fourth, they consent. This is the hardest one for Western readers. The elements decide who they will speak with. You can light a thousand candles and never hear from fire if fire does not want to speak with you. Practice does not entitle you to relationship. Practice makes you available for relationship. Whether the elements show up is up to the elements.

I have students who have done daily candle vigils for two years and report no contact. I have students who lit a candle for the first time three months ago and now describe fire as their primary teacher. I cannot explain the difference. I do not think the difference is about merit. I think it is about something the elements know that I do not, and so the practice I will teach you in this book is the practice of becoming available, not the practice of guaranteeing contact.

If contact does not come, the practice is still good. The practice will change you, and what it changes you toward is the kind of person to whom contact, when it comes, will not break you. I have seen what happens to practitioners for whom contact comes too soon. I do not want that for you.

What you will do in the next twelve chapters

The book is structured as a year of practice. Each chapter is meant to be read in the first week of a lunation, and then practiced for the rest of the lunation, before moving on. If you read the book straight through in three days, you will have absorbed some of the worldview, but you will not have done the work. Please do the work.

The first three chapters set the frame. Chapter Two is a brief history of how the Western tradition forgot that the elements were beings, and what it cost. Chapter Three is the practical chapter on protocol, offering, and listening, the three disciplines that govern every animist relationship. Read these slowly. They are the foundation.

Chapters Four through Seven are the four elemental chapters: fire, water, earth, air. Each one has its own structure, its own plant ally, its own daily practice, and its own ceremony at the end. Do not skip any of them. If you only care about fire, read about water anyway. The elements know each other. They will know if you have only studied one of them.

Chapter Eight steps beyond the four, into the fifth element and the elements beyond it. Chapter Nine returns to ethics. Chapters Ten and Eleven are the most practical chapters in the book: how to build a home altar that will hold up across years, and how to design and lead a ceremony of your own.

Chapter Twelve is about what comes after. What it looks like to live with the elements as relationships rather than as topics. This is the chapter I have most needed to write and the chapter I am least sure I have gotten right. I will keep writing it for the rest of my life.

What I am not going to do in this book

I am not going to teach you to call on the elements for personal power. The elements are not a battery. They are not a tool. They will not, in my experience, make you wealthy or beautiful or successful, and if you go to them asking for these things, you will mostly get silence, and the silence is informative.

I am not going to teach you ceremony with psychoactive plants. This is not a book about ayahuasca, peyote, mushrooms, or any of the other sacraments that have proper teachers in their proper lineages. I have my own relationships with some of these plants, and I will mention them in the appropriate plant-ally sections, but I will not give you protocols for working with them. That work requires a teacher in the room. A book cannot do it.

I am not going to teach you to channel beings whose lineage I do not know. I am not going to teach you forms of practice that I have not done myself for at least seven years. I am not going to teach you anything I learned from a teacher who has not been dead at least one full year, because the dead can correct what I get wrong and the living deserve to be paid for their teachings directly. This is my own protocol. You may develop a different one.

What I am asking of you, in the next twenty-four hours

Find a candle. White or beeswax if possible. Not a scented candle.

Find a small saucer or plate that you can dedicate to this candle for the next four days.

Find a window that faces east, if your home has one. If not, any window will do.

Tomorrow morning, when you wake up, before you reach for your phone, place the candle on the saucer, place the saucer near the window, and light the candle. Sit with it. Do not pray. Do not meditate in any technique you have been taught. Just sit. Watch.

After ten minutes, blow the candle out. Get up. Continue your day.

Do this for four mornings.

On the fifth morning, before you light the candle, ask it, silently or aloud, whether it is willing to keep meeting you.

Listen for the answer.

Then come back to Chapter Two.

Sample Chapter Four

Fire: The First Conversation

The Mexican curanderas I learned from in East LA do not begin with fire. They begin with water, with the limpia, the cleansing. They wash before they speak. This is good and proper protocol, and if you are working with a curandera, you will follow her order, not mine.

In this book I begin with fire. I begin with fire for two reasons. The first is that fire is the most accessible element for the modern Western practitioner. A candle is the most domestic of magical objects. We have grown up with fire on birthday cakes and on dinner tables and at the ends of incense sticks. We have a relationship with fire already, even if we have not noticed it.

The second reason is that fire is the most willing of the four. In my experience, fire is the element that most reliably responds when a serious practitioner first calls. I have seen this with my students again and again. The candle leans, the flame draws toward the practitioner, the wax behaves in ways that no draft can account for. Fire shows up.

I do not know why. I have asked. The answer I most often get, when I sit at the candle and ask fire why fire is so willing to teach, is something like: because the others are harder. Which I take to mean: fire is the door, and the door has been opened to the modern practitioner because the modern practitioner is, frankly, in trouble, and the elements have decided to make themselves available.

Who fire has been

Before we begin practice with fire, you should know who you are meeting. Fire is older than human language and older than any tradition that names it. But within every animist tradition there are specific named keepers of fire-relationship, and you should know a few of them by name as a courtesy.

In the Mediterranean and European traditions, Hestia is the Greek keeper of the hearth fire and the eldest of the Olympians, and her presence inside a home is the difference between a house and a home. Brigid is her Celtic cousin, the threefold keeper of the forge, the hearth, and the inspiration, and the Catholic figure of Saint Brigid of Kildare is the surviving form of the older Brigid, with her perpetual flame at Kildare. The Vesta cult of Rome maintained an undying flame at the center of the city, tended by women who were both honored and constrained.

In the Mexican tradition I trained in, fire is Abuelo Fuego, grandfather fire, and fire ceremonies are held in the temazcal (sweat lodge) and at the center of the danza circle. The Mexican curandera tradition treats fire as both purifier and witness: fire sees what the human does and remembers it. The fire ceremonies of the Nahuatl, Mexica, and Huichol traditions are alive today, and they are not mine to teach. I name them so that you know they exist and so that you do not think that what I am about to teach you is the totality of fire-practice.

In the African diaspora, the Orisha tradition holds fire in the figure of Shango, the warrior of justice, with his thunderaxe and his drums. Shango is also not mine to teach. If you are drawn to the Orisha tradition, you should find an initiated practitioner. I name Shango here because fire-as-being is recognized across these traditions, and the recognition is the point.

There are others. I will not list all of them. The point is: fire has been known by many people, by many names, for a very long time. When you sit at the candle, you are not the first.

What fire prefers

Fire prefers honesty. This is the first thing I learned that I think is broadly true across traditions. Fire dislikes performance and dislikes flattery. If you sit at the candle and recite words that do not mean anything to you, fire will not respond. Fire will simply burn, which is what it does anyway.

Fire prefers to be alone with you. Lighting a candle in the middle of a crowded room while music plays is not, in my experience, the way to begin a relationship with fire. Fire is patient with social fires (the dinner candle, the birthday candle) and treats them as background, the way a person at a party treats the background music. The relationship that fire offers you is not in those candles. It is in the candle you light when you are alone, in a quiet room, in a deliberate way.

Fire prefers offerings that are real. The simplest offerings, in the Mexican tradition I learned, are tobacco (real loose-leaf tobacco, not cigarette tobacco) and copal (the resin, not the synthetic incense). A small pinch of either, placed in a fire-safe container near the candle, is an honorable beginning. Other traditions offer different substances. In Brigid's tradition, you might offer milk and butter and the first beer of a brewing. In the Vesta tradition, you might offer salted barley. Choose what fits your lineage, and if you have no lineage, choose what fits where you live.

Fire prefers a candle you have prepared. The candles I use for serious fire-work are not pulled out of a drawer. They are dressed (the wax is anointed with a small amount of olive oil or a personally significant oil), they are dedicated (named, silently or aloud, for the work they will do), and they are kept on a fire-safe dish that is used only for this practice. The candle that you use for chapter four should not be the candle you use for dinner. The discipline of separation is part of the protocol.

How fire communicates

There are at least four ways I know that fire communicates with a sitting practitioner.

Through the behavior of the flame. Fire moves. It leans. It elongates, it sputters, it grows, it flickers in patterns that, with time, you can learn to read. I keep a fire journal. Across years, the journal has shown me that a particular kind of upward-stretching flame appears when I have asked a question fire is willing to answer at length, and a particular kind of horizontal flame appears when fire is impatient. I do not assume that everyone's journal will say the same thing. I assume that everyone's flame says something to them, and the practice is to begin paying attention.

Through the behavior of the wax. Wax pools. It runs. It crystallizes in ridges. After a sustained sitting, the wax often forms shapes. I do not interpret these the way I have seen some divination traditions interpret them. I treat them as signature. Fire was here. The wax remembers.

Through smoke and scent. Even a beeswax candle, which has very little smoke, will at certain moments release a stronger scent. The scent is information. I cannot tell you what the information means in advance. You will learn what the information means by sitting with it for months.

Through your own body. This is the channel most modern practitioners trust least, and it is the channel that is the most reliable in the long run. While you sit at the candle, your body will respond. Your heart rate will change. Your breath will deepen or quicken. You will notice an impulse to stand up, or an impulse to weep, or an impulse to laugh, or an impulse to sit in absolute stillness. These are not, in my view, your reactions to fire. They are fire's communication routed through the most available instrument it has, which is your nervous system. Trust the body.

The plant ally for fire: yerba santa

In the bioregion this book is written from (Southern California and the Southwest), the plant most often paired with fire ceremony is yerba santa, Eriodictyon californicum. Yerba santa is a chaparral shrub, with sticky leaves that smell like resin and old smoke when crushed. It is sacred to the Chumash and the Tongva, on whose land Los Angeles sits, and it has been used in fire and breath ceremonies in this region for thousands of years.

If you wildcraft yerba santa, do so on land where the plant grows freely, take no more than 10% of any individual plant, leave an offering, and do not strip a young plant of its first-year leaves. Better, in my view, is to buy from a respectful grower. A small amount goes a long way. I burn three to five leaves over the candle once a week and no more.

If you live outside the bioregion, choose a fire-corresponding plant native to where you live, with the same care. In the Mediterranean, rosemary is honorable. In the British Isles, gorse. In the Eastern US, cedar (with the same sourcing care as yerba santa). The element is fire; the plant is the local relative.

The 28-day practice

Here is the practice for this lunation.

Days 1 through 7: Sit. Every morning, before your phone, light the candle on the dedicated dish, place a small offering (tobacco, copal, or your tradition's equivalent) in the offering container, and sit with the candle for 15 to 20 minutes. Do not pray. Do not ask for anything. Do not interpret what you see. Just sit. After the sitting, blow out the candle and continue your day. In the evening, before you sleep, write three sentences in your fire journal: what the flame did, what the wax did, what your body did.

Days 8 through 14: Ask. Every morning, same practice, but in the seventh minute of the sitting, ask fire one question. Silently or aloud. Keep the question simple: What do you want me to know? or Will you teach me? or What am I missing? Do not expect an answer. Continue the journal.

Days 15 through 21: Offer. Every morning, same practice. In the third minute of the sitting, place the offering deliberately, name what you are offering and why. I offer this tobacco in gratitude for the warmth this house has held this winter. Or whatever is true. Do not flatter. Do not perform.

Days 22 through 28: Sustained sitting. Extend the morning sitting to 40 minutes, no longer. In one of these seven mornings, you may notice a shift in the quality of attention. You may not. Both are acceptable. On day 28, after the morning sitting, write a longer entry in your fire journal: what you have learned, what you suspect you still do not know, what you want to ask in the next lunation.

What to do if you feel nothing in 28 days

Some readers will sit for 28 mornings and feel nothing remarkable. They will not see flame behavior they cannot explain. They will not feel shifts in the body. They will write twenty-eight unremarkable journal entries. They will be tempted to conclude that they are not suited for this work, or that I am making it up, or that the elements have rejected them.

None of these conclusions is, in my experience, correct.

The most common cause of "nothing happening" in the first month is that the practitioner is performing the practice rather than entering it. The body knows the difference between a discipline you are doing because a book told you to and a relationship you are entering because you mean it. Fire knows the difference too. The remedy, if you have done 28 unremarkable mornings, is not to try harder. It is to ask yourself, honestly, why you came to this candle in the first place.

If the answer is because I wanted to be a special kind of practitioner, fire will be silent. Fire is not impressed.

If the answer is because I am lonely for something my culture has stopped offering me, fire will, eventually, answer.

The honest answer earns the answer back. The performed answer earns the silence back. Both are forms of teaching.

Moving to the next chapter

When you are ready to move to chapter five, leave the fire candle lit one morning of your choosing, sit a full 40 minutes, and at the end of the sitting, place your hand briefly above the flame (not in it). Thank fire. Blow the candle out. Take the candle off the dedicated dish and put it somewhere private. You will return to it.

Then go to water.

The Living Elements · Lesson 3

The three disciplines

  1. Protocol. The rules of approach. The honorable default: ask permission, listen for response, wait, do not proceed without permission.
  2. Offering. Always real. Honorably sourced. Small is honorable; large is suspicious. Your breath, voice, and time are the most fundamental offerings.
  3. Listening. The receptive discipline. The hardest. The body is the primary instrument.

What consent looks like

A "yes" feels like opening. A relaxation in the body. The flame steadies, the water clarifies, the wind softens, the earth supports.

A "no" feels like closing. A tightening. The flame guts out, the water muddies. Or simply: nothing. A flat absence.

When you receive a "no," accept it. Try again another day, with a different protocol or offering. Do not push.

The water practice for the next 14 days

Plant ally for water: rose

Wild rose if you can find it. Damask rose petals from a culinary source if not. Avoid commercial florist roses.
